\begin{abstract}
\noindent I reanalyze the 248-village evaluation of Catholic Relief Services' Gikuriro program in Rwanda to distinguish fitted dietary gains from protection against a poor allocation. At its expected provider budget, a lower/large-cash lottery exceeds Gikuriro by \GKFittedRegret\ fitted food groups among identified diets; a positive full-baseline advantage remains unresolved. Combining missing-diet intervals, common score distributions and conditional assignment quotas gives a mean-loss bound of \QuotaMeanLoss\ groups. Exact regional lower certificates place this allocation within \QuotaRegionalMeanGap\ groups of the optimum in the implemented outer region. Further allocation search therefore cannot substantially narrow this particular guarantee. Its optimized regional loss is over 0.665 groups below a weight-sensitive hybrid's lower certificate, conditional on their respective constructions. That ordering does not imply lower actual regret: a restricted empirical-Bernstein rule performs better in important parts of a known-population comparison. These results locate the limits of the specified protection and distinguish them from optimization error, general statistical impossibility and the organization's unidentified welfare ranking.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} food security; Catholic Relief Services; cash transfers; dietary diversity; treatment allocation.\\

\section{Introduction}

A food-security organization must decide both what to provide and how many households to reach. Giving a large transfer to a small share of villages can improve recipients' diets while leaving most villages untreated. A less expensive package can reach everyone and produce a smaller gain per recipient. An experimental comparison of package means informs this choice only after the untreated households, the provider budget, and uncertainty about the outcomes enter the same calculation.

I examine this question in the Gikuriro program in Rwanda. Catholic Relief Services (CRS) and SNV implemented the program with support from USAID. Nutrition and health activities, agricultural support, saving groups, and water, sanitation, and hygiene interventions formed a single package. Its stated aim was to improve maternal and child nutrition. The experiment of \citet{mcintosh2024} assigned 248 villages to Gikuriro, control, or one of four cash amounts delivered by GiveDirectly. The title recalls Mark 6:37. Religious affiliation was not randomized, so the experiment cannot identify a specifically Catholic delivery mechanism.

The parent study already develops equal-cost cash benchmarking, cost effectiveness, and the tradeoff between coverage and intensity. For mean diet, the Gikuriro comparison against a control/large-cash lottery is exactly its cost-effectiveness contrast multiplied by Gikuriro's cost. Its studentized statistic is unchanged. I therefore ask a narrower question: what allocation can be defended when the objective includes the diet distribution, missing outcomes are allowed to differ by assignment, and the organization can diversify across packages? The economic distinction is between the fitted winner and the opportunity cost that the evidence can credibly bound.

At the source's expected provider budget of \$\GikuriroCost\ per eligible household, large cash alone is unaffordable. A control/large lottery serves \LargeCoverage\ percent of villages. A lower/large lottery serves every village with cash and reserves the large package for \LowerLargeShare\ percent. The latter has the highest fitted mean dietary diversity. Choosing Gikuriro instead has fitted loss \GKFittedRegret\ groups on the twelve-group household score. Missing-outcome endpoint means, however, allow the sign of this contrast to reverse. The stronger observed assignment response to large cash does not resolve the equal-budget decision.

The decision class itself supplies protection even before any outcomes are used. With only the twelve-group support and the source costs, the optimized worst-case mean loss is \SupportMeanLoss\ groups. Assigning equal shares to the five individually affordable packages attains this bound. Its improvement over selecting a single package is diversification under ignorance, rather than information learned from the experiment. I report this benchmark before evaluating what the observed diets add.

Observed-outcome consistency tightens the bound to \LogicalMeanLoss\ groups for the fixed weighted released baseline sample. A finite conditional-assignment calculation adds simultaneous intervals for mean, survival and shortfall targets. Requiring those intervals to describe one score distribution per arm, and intersecting them with consistency, gives \TightMeanLoss\ groups. Relative to support alone, the total tightening is \TotalInformationTightening\ groups, or \TotalInformationPercent\ percent. Neither this conservative result nor the previously looser mean box establishes that all valid analyses must be uninformative. The new projection demonstrates that the same outcomes and coverage event can support a stronger calculation.

Using the quota geometry more directly gives a verified mean-loss bound of \QuotaMeanLoss\ groups. I compute a conservative exponential-moment maximum over the bounded village populations using a sorted-threshold recurrence. A single sum-of-exponentials event then protects the common diet distributions without treating their uncertainty as six independent interval budgets. The resulting allocation spends \$\QuotaAllocationCost\ per eligible household. Its bound is \QuotaMeanImprovement\ groups below the earlier coherent construction. This comparison concerns two separately valid conditional procedures; it does not allocate a common coverage event to their intersection or select their smaller realized bound. The headline result uses the quota procedure alone. The calculation adapts established symmetric-convex and sampling arguments; no new general inference theorem or general range algorithm is claimed.

The central regional result separates allocation search from uncertainty about outcomes. Exact feasible adversarial distributions and a policy dual put the quota region's optimized mean loss between \QuotaRegionalMeanLower\ and \QuotaRegionalMeanUpper\ groups. The selected allocation is therefore within \QuotaRegionalMeanGap\ groups of this regional optimum. A weight-sensitive product/harmonic hybrid has a regional lower certificate above 6.532 groups: optimizing its allocation cannot close its gap of more than 0.665 groups with the quota proposal in these implemented regions. The constructions therefore provide different optimized regional protection. Neither bracket measures actual regret or the sharp information content of the experiment. Nearly optimal allocation within a conservative region can still leave a broad guarantee.

The performance comparison limits that claim further. Across a declared grid of two-arm, equally costly known populations, the fixed-forecast empirical-Bernstein rule gives lower average actual regret than quota with complete and coarse reports. I report effect-specific results, paired Monte Carlo uncertainty and two simple rules without comparable certificates. A no-learning half allocation has exact average regret 0.045 across the four effects. The empirical-best-arm rule uses reported-interval midpoints, including a specified midpoint for missing scores. These comparisons preserve the negative evidence: tighter field regional protection is not a general ranking of decision rules. The constructed grid conditions on particular populations and reporting patterns; its averages are not estimates for Rwanda or a distribution of policy problems.

The much smaller exploratory block bound, \ApproxMeanAllocation\ groups, remains unvalidated. Fixed quotas induce dependence across arms within 22 assignment blocks. Common block scores retain that covariance, but synthetic fifteen-comparison coverage is still \EqualWeightCoverage\ with equal weights and \UnequalWeightCoverage\ with unequal frame weights. Those failures falsify a general nominal-coverage interpretation of the approximation; they do not estimate its unknown coverage for Rwanda. The finite event instead assumes exchangeable labels conditional on each released block's counts, independent blocks and fixed baseline weights. It covers that released target, not unsampled households or deployment by itself.\footnote{The public AEA entry AEARCTR-0002559 confirms computer randomization but does not specify all assignment restrictions; its analysis plan requires access permission. The accepted parent paper describes the 22 district/poverty blocks. Neither source verifies the uniform conditional law used here. The documentation audit records the registry's inconsistent arm counts and registration timing.}

The relevant opportunity cost also depends on the organization's rules. Eliminating control raises the smallest mean-loss bound against the original menu from \TightMeanLoss\ to \UniversalCommonLoss. Comparing only among universally assisted allocations produces a smaller regret number because the benchmark changes. Similarly, requiring a Gikuriro share can mechanically scale regret when it restricts both the choice and comparator. I distinguish these effects rather than treating a smaller within-menu bound as evidence that an assistance mandate improves diet. This is a change in the optimized worst-case bound, rather than a measured dietary opportunity cost. The rules are hypothetical institutional sensitivities, not observed CRS requirements.

The decision problem draws directly on \citet{manski2004,manski2007} and \citet{stoye2007}. Their work makes missing outcomes and multiple treatments part of treatment choice. \citet{kitagawa2018} studies empirical welfare maximization in richer policy classes. Here I minimize a confidence-region bound after observing the data. I do not establish a rule that minimizes worst-case expected regret over repeated samples. The contribution is a conditional empirical comparison that identifies how much of a broad allocation guarantee can be attributed to optimization within specified regions, and tests whether better protection carries over to actual decisions in known populations. The underlying treatment-choice logic is established. The empirical core remains the Gikuriro assignment contrast and its measured-diet consequences; broader welfare and organizational choice require information this experiment does not supply.

The measured objective remains limited. Household dietary diversity describes current food access, while child nutrition, future resources, and public services may rank aid differently. \citet{curriegahvari2008} reviews reasons for in-kind provision; \citet{banerjeeduflo2007} documents spending priorities beyond food; and \citet{haushofer2016} studies cash effects on consumption, assets, and food security. The local price and nonrecipient responses in \citet{cunha2019,egger2022} further limit transport of an original-delivery lottery. An illustrative saving model in the appendix explains how intertemporal utility can rise without a current dietary gain. The bundled experiment supplies no variation identifying that mechanism separately.

\section{Decision problem and theoretical framework}

Let $a\in\mathcal A=\{0,g,\ell,m,u,L\}$ denote control, Gikuriro, and the four cash packages. Let $D_i(a)$ be the diet of baseline household $i$ when its village receives assignment $a$, at the original within-village saturation. A higher value of a specified transformation $v(D)$ is preferred. I consider the mean $v(d)=d$, all integer survival indicators, and negative linear shortfalls
\begin{equation}
 v_z(d)=-\frac{(z-d)_+}{z},\qquad z=1,\ldots,12.\label{eq:shortfall}
\end{equation}
The thresholds are alternative priorities over a household food-access proxy. They are not official hunger cutoffs. The shortfall structure follows \citet{foster1984}; it does not assign nutritional equivalence to all food groups.

For fixed positive baseline weights $w_i$, define $\mu_a(v)=\sum_iw_i v(D_i(a))/\sum_iw_i$ over the released baseline eligible sample. This target conditions on sample inclusion and its weights. Treating it as the mean of the entire eligible frame additionally requires the household sampling and observation assumptions. For policy deployment, I consider comparable villages with the same eligible-household weighting and package delivery. That transport restriction is maintained, not tested by random assignment.

A village lottery chooses $q_a\geq0$ with $\sum_aq_a=1$, independently of potential outcomes conditional on target blocks. With fixed provider costs $c_a$ and $c_0=0$, the feasible set is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal Q(b)=\{q\geq0:\ \boldsymbol1'q=1,\ c'q\leq b\}.\label{eq:planner}
\end{equation}
The source costs are standardized average costs per eligible household. In particular, cash overhead is costed separately as if each package served 56,127 households across eight districts, a national scale comparable to Gikuriro. These accounts do not observe the marginal cost of a small package share. Equation \eqref{eq:planner} conditions on their proportional allocation and stable delivery. It imposes an expected cost per eligible household; it does not ensure an exact realized cap for finitely many villages of unequal size.

\begin{proposition}[Observed-package lotteries]\label{prop:vertices}
Under stable package outcomes and no cross-village interference, a lottery has $F_q(d)=\sum_aq_aF_a(d)$ and value $q'\mu(v)$. For known $\mu(v)$, a maximizer over $\mathcal Q(b)$ exists with at most two packages in its support.
\end{proposition}

When $c_a<b<c_h$, the binding two-package vertex has
\begin{equation}
 q_h=\frac{b-c_a}{c_h-c_a},\qquad q_a=1-q_h.\label{eq:mix}
\end{equation}
Every pure feasible package is also considered, including policies that leave funds unspent. The specified objective assigns no value to unused resources or benefits beyond the diet transformation. It therefore describes a hypothetical measured-diet benchmark, not an estimated institutional welfare function. Below I examine a binding expected-spending rule and alternative explicit values of unspent resources. At $b=c_g$, the full six-package set has nine vertices: pure Gikuriro and eight cash/control vertices. The latter are control, the three small pure cash packages, and four cheaper-package/large-cash mixtures. A budget change requires recomputing the vertices, including cost-order reversals.

\begin{corollary}[Coverage comparison]\label{cor:coverage}
Universal Gikuriro weakly exceeds the control/large lottery for objective $v$ precisely when
\[
 \mu_g-\mu_0\geq(c_g/c_L)(\mu_L-\mu_0).
\]
For mean diet, the difference equals $c_g\{\tau_g/c_g-\tau_L/c_L\}$, the rescaled parent-study cost-effectiveness comparison.
\end{corollary}

For uncertain arm means in a nonempty compact region $\mathcal C$, define
\begin{equation}
 R(q,\mu)=\max_{r\in\mathcal Q(b)}(r-q)'\mu,\qquad
 U_{\mathcal C}(q)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal C}R(q,\mu).\label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
Regret measures lost expected value in the specified diet objective. If $\mathcal C$ covers the common true means, $U_{\mathcal C}(q)$ bounds their regret. A selected allocation may therefore use the same event without selecting a separate test after fitting it.

\begin{proposition}[Diversification over a common region]\label{prop:allocation}
Let $\mathcal V$ be the vertices of $\mathcal Q(b)$. Then
\[
 U_{\mathcal C}(q)=\max_{r\in\mathcal V}\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal C}(r-q)'\mu
\]
is convex in $q$. A minimizer over $\mathcal Q(b)$ exists and need not be a vertex. If $\mathcal C=\{\mu:A\mu\leq d\}$ is compact, the minimum is obtained from a linear program with a nonnegative dual vector for each comparator vertex. On a simultaneous coverage event, its selected allocation has regret at most its reported bound.
\end{proposition}

For example, two treatments with costs below budget and independent mean intervals $[0,1]$ have worst-case loss $\max(q,1-q)$ when $q$ is the share given the first. The best vertex has loss one and the equal mixture has loss one half. Proposition \ref{prop:vertices} therefore limits the comparator under known means; it does not restrict the choice minimizing \eqref{eq:regret}. This elementary illustration is established decision logic, not a new theorem about treatment allocation.

The fitted maximizer uses $\widehat\mu$ as if known. The region minimizer instead protects against every mean vector admitted by $\mathcal C$. Neither procedure here is proved statistical minimax regret in the repeated-sampling sense. The region is an outer set rather than the sharp set of all compatible potential outcomes and distributions. Its bound can be conservative even when its coverage is valid.

\section{Setting, sources, and measurement}

The baseline survey took place in August--September 2016 and endline in September--October 2017. Gikuriro included village nutrition schools, farmer field learning schools, agricultural inputs, small livestock, Savings and Internal Lending Communities, and environmental health promotion. Eligibility combined poverty classification with a young child or a pregnant or lactating woman. Cash assignment operated at village level and covered the source's eligible beneficiaries. Assignment effects include receipt, nonreceipt, and within-village consequences under original delivery.

The parent paper describes computer randomization in 22 district/poverty blocks of 10--13 villages. The released counts give 74 Gikuriro, 74 control, 22 each lower, middle, and upper cash, and 34 large-cash villages. Each small cash arm has one village in every block. The inspected public replication scripts contain no original assignment program establishing every randomization restriction. I assume labels are exchangeable conditional on the released block counts and assignments are independent across those blocks. Uniform quota assignment implies this model; additional assignment restrictions need not. The probabilities used below are $p_{ba}=n_{ba}/n_b$ under that explicit conditional model, rather than known solely because realized frequencies can be calculated. The public registry and its count discrepancy are documented in the replication design note; they do not resolve this missing assignment information.

I use the corrected CC BY 4.0 release of \citet{data2025}, DOI \href{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15881329}{10.5281/zenodo.15881329}. \subsection{One distribution for each arm}

For an identified transformation $Y_i=v(D_i)$ and its observation indicator $R_i$, define
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\mu_a=\frac{\sum_i T_{j(i),a}w_iR_iY_i/p_{b(i),a}}
 {\sum_i T_{j(i),a}w_iR_i/p_{b(i),a}}.\label{eq:hajek}
\end{equation}
The weights are positive and fixed at baseline. They numerically equal the eligible village frame count divided by its released baseline sample count, within floating-point precision. I invent no intensive-tracking factor. For identified diets, \eqref{eq:hajek} targets the weighted potential-observation ratio under each arm. Different assignment-specific observation can change its composition. An observed-diet policy calculation is consequently a sensitivity measure; it represents policy diet for the baseline target only with an additional selection assumption.

For the baseline outcome, I replace $Y_i$ by $v(D_i^-)$ or $v(D_i^+)$ and set $R_i=1$ for every baseline eligible. Since every reported $v$ is increasing, these endpoint means bound the common true arm mean. A contrast uses lower endpoints for positive coefficients and upper endpoints for negative coefficients to form its lower bound, with the assignments reversed for its upper bound. The resulting estimated endpoint region is subject to sampling uncertainty. It is not already a confidence interval for missing diets.

Applying \eqref{eq:hajek} to indicators gives a normalized empirical CDF. Its means and shortfalls satisfy
\[
 \widehat\E[D]=\sum_{k=1}^{12}\widehat\Pr(D\geq k),\qquad
 \widehat\E[(z-D)_+]=\sum_{k=0}^{z-1}\widehat\Pr(D\leq k)
\]
exactly. The mean, twelve survivals, and twelve linear shortfalls have two affine duplicates, leaving 23 distinct transforms. The analysis covers increasing and increasing-concave priorities on the bounded integer score. Ratio normalization ensures coherence; it does not make finite-sample ratios exactly unbiased.

\subsection{Finite conditional-assignment outer regions}

Let $W_j=\sum_{i\in j}w_i$ be the fixed eligible baseline weight in village $j$, $W_b^{\max}=\max_{j\in b}W_j$, and $k_{ba}=n_{ba}$. For a primitive transformation supported on $[L,H]$, let $r=H-L$ and
\begin{equation}
 V_a=\sum_b k_{ba}\left(\frac{W_b^{\max}}{p_{ba}}\right)^2,
 \qquad t_a=r\sqrt{\tfrac12 V_a\log(2M/\alpha)}.\label{eq:finite}
\end{equation}
Here $M$ is the number of primitive arm/endpoints protected together. If $\widehat D_a$ is the denominator of \eqref{eq:hajek}, the interval $[\widehat\mu_a-t_a/\widehat D_a,\widehat\mu_a+t_a/\widehat D_a]\cap[L,H]$ covers its fixed conditional target with probability at least $1-\alpha/M$. A zero denominator gives full support. Appendix \ref{app:proofs} proves this through bounded sampling without replacement using \citet{hoeffding1963}.

I use $M=276$ for six arms, 23 transforms, and two full-baseline endpoints, with $\alpha=0.05$. A separate identified-outcome family uses $M=138$. These are separate 95 percent events. Their conjunction is not asserted to have 95 percent coverage. The same primitive event covers every transformation used in the coherent distributional projection below. Retaining only mean intervals would discard protected information. For an unobserved diet, the lower endpoint's lower confidence bound and upper endpoint's upper bound enclose the true mean. This finite statement conditions on the exchangeable assignment model, the fixed weights, and stable potential outcomes. It does not prove full-frame sampling representativeness, transport, or a hard budget guarantee.

\subsection{Outcome consistency and coherent distributional regions}

Write $p_{ad}$ for arm $a$'s weighted potential probability at integer score $d=0,\ldots,12$. For the baseline target, let $L_{av}$ be the finite lower bound for $v(D^-)$ and $U_{av}$ the finite upper bound for $v(D^+)$. Define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P_a=\left\{p_a\geq0:\ \sum_dp_{ad}=1,\quad
 L_{av}\leq\sum_dv(d)p_{ad}\leq U_{av}\ \text{for every protected }v\right\}.\label{eq:distributionregion}
\end{equation}
Every true arm distribution satisfies these inequalities on the original 276-primitive event. The separate 138-target family can be used for potential identified-diet ratios because all their transformations use the same diet-observation indicator. A transformation known despite missing items does not convert an unidentified diet into an observed one.

Consistency gives additional deterministic restrictions. Let $W=\sum_iw_i$ and let $S_a$ contain households whose village actually receives $a$. For increasing $v$ with support $[L_v,H_v]$,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\sum_{i\in S_a}w_iv(D_i^-)+L_v\sum_{i\notin S_a}w_i}{W}
 \leq\mu_a(v)\leq
 \frac{\sum_{i\in S_a}w_iv(D_i^+)+H_v\sum_{i\notin S_a}w_i}{W}.\label{eq:consistency}
\end{equation}
These inequalities use no assignment probabilities. They leave counterfactual outcomes unrestricted on their support and rely on consistency of the reported score with the observed food items. I impose them for every transformation in \eqref{eq:distributionregion}. A scalar consistency bound can be sharp while the resulting joint moment polytope is only an outer set. I make no general joint-sharpness claim.

\begin{proposition}[Using the protected distribution]\label{prop:projection}
Suppose all protected primitive limits cover their arm targets simultaneously. Intersecting \eqref{eq:distributionregion} with valid consistency restrictions preserves that event. For a specified $v$, minimize and maximize $\sum_dv(d)p_{ad}$ within each arm polytope. Their product encloses the common arm means without a new multiplicity penalty. It is the exact scalar projection of this arm-separable outer polytope. The selected allocation's loss bound from Proposition \ref{prop:allocation} remains valid on the same event.
\end{proposition}

The optimization fails explicitly if a distribution polytope is empty. It never drops conflicting constraints to produce a certificate. Each projection retains feasible endpoint distributions, and an independent epigraph program over the projected box's 64 corners verifies the allocation calculation. This projection is a use of existing information, not a new confidence procedure. Moment-wise consistency and the finite event remain conservative, and other justified procedures may extract more.

\subsection{A shared quota-moment region}

The preceding construction first assigns a separate interval to every protected target and then projects their intersection. I also use a single exponential budget for all packages and transformations. For a fixed increasing score function $z(d)\in[0,1]$, let $y_j$ be its weighted potential village mean and $u_a$ its full weighted baseline mean. For a uniform subset $A_b$ of $k_{ba}$ out of $n_b$ villages, define the centered block residual
\[
 G_b(y,u_a)=\sum_{j\in b}\left(\frac{\ind\{j\in A_b\}}{p_{ba}}-1\right)W_j(y_j-u_a).
\]
The across-block sum equals the arm's Horvitz--Thompson residual because $\sum_jW_j(y_j-u_a)=0$. For any $\lambda_a\geq0$, an outcome-free log-moment upper bound is
\begin{equation}
 B_{ba}(\lambda_a)=\log\max_{y\in\{0,1\}^{n_b},\,u\in\{0,1\}}
 \binom{n_b}{k_{ba}}^{-1}\sum_{|A_b|=k_{ba}}\exp\{\lambda_aG_b(y,u)\},\qquad B_a=\sum_bB_{ba}.\label{eq:quotamoment}
\end{equation}
The maximum over binary vertices bounds every bounded village population by convexity. It deliberately relaxes the relation between the village outcomes and their true overall mean. Symmetry reduces the populations to sorted thresholds, and a positive dynamic program averages the quota subsets implicitly (Appendix \ref{app:quotacomputation}). This computes the relaxed moment without enumerating exponentially many subsets or claiming a sharp least-favorable potential-outcome population. Complementing $y$ and $u$ reverses the residual, so the same constant bounds its negative sign. This does not assert symmetry of any individual unequal-weight population's sampling distribution.

For each of 23 distinct normalized score functions, let $N_{az}^-$ and $N_{az}^+$ be the assignment-weighted observed endpoint sums, and $\widehat D_a$ the full-baseline assignment-weighted denominator. Define
\begin{equation}
 e_{az}^+(p_a)=\exp\{\lambda_a(N_{az}^--\widehat D_a z'p_a)-B_a\},\qquad
 e_{az}^-(p_a)=\exp\{-\lambda_a(N_{az}^+-\widehat D_a z'p_a)-B_a\}.\label{eq:quotatests}
\end{equation}
Each term has expectation at most one at the true arm distribution, even when missing diets differ by assignment. Summing the 276 one-sided terms and applying Markov's inequality gives the following event.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional quota mixture]\label{prop:quota}
Maintain uniform quota assignment within blocks, independent blocks, fixed positive baseline weights and consistent bounded potential diets. With probability at least 0.95, the true six score distributions lie in
\[
 \mathcal C_Q=\left\{(p_a)_a:\ p_a\in\mathcal P_a^{\rm obs},\quad
 \sum_{a,z,\sigma\in\{+,-\}}e_{az}^{\sigma}(p_a)\leq5520\right\},
\]
where $\mathcal P_a^{\rm obs}$ imposes unit mass, nonnegativity, deterministic outcome consistency and observed identified-score bin floors. Every data-selected feasible allocation has regret bounded by a valid support upper bound over this common region.
\end{proposition}

The exponential scale is chosen on the fixed grid $\{1,1.25,1.5,2\}\sqrt{8\log(5520)/V_a}$ by minimizing $\{B_a(\lambda)+\log(5520)\}/\lambda$. Only baseline weights, quotas and the stated family enter that choice. Allocation shares and support dual variables may use observed outcomes because Proposition \ref{prop:quota} protects every allocation on one event. The new event is separate from the earlier 276-primitive and 138-identified-target events. I do not intersect their nominal 95 percent sets and retain that level.

The code interprets normalized score entries as fixed stored bounded functions, encloses moment and observation arithmetic outward, and projects the resulting probability region onto the exact mean and six-group shortfall objectives. For each of the nine ideal cost vertices, a nonnegative Lagrange multiplier splits the shared exponential budget into six convex arm problems. Enclosed tangents and feasible LP duals give support upper bounds regardless of whether the numerical candidate minimizes its convex problem. The reported shares are outcome-selected exchange-search proposals with verified loss bounds; their search gaps are numerical diagnostics, rather than certified minimax optimality. Appendix \ref{app:proofs} gives the coverage and numerical-bound arguments. The package retains the proposal, constants, tangents and dual witnesses for direct replay.

\subsection{Exploratory block approximation and stress tests}

For block $b$ and arm $a$, let $N_{ba}$ and $D_{ba}$ be the numerator and denominator contributions in \eqref{eq:hajek}. The ratio score is
\begin{equation}
 u_{ba}=\frac{N_{ba}-\widehat\mu_aD_{ba}}{\sum_hD_{ha}},\qquad
 \widehat\Sigma=\frac{B}{B-1}\sum_bu_bu_b',\quad B=22.\label{eq:blockcov}
\end{equation}
Grouping before covariance retains dependence among all arms assigned within a block. Arm-exclusive village scores would force cross-arm covariance to zero. Appendix \ref{app:proofs} gives an exact counterexample: that omission estimates only 90 percent of the true difference variance in a ten-village quota design, even as the number of identical blocks increases. The common block covariance repairs this omission. Its asymptotic justification additionally requires increasing independent blocks, stable positive denominator limits, and no dominating block.

Shared block Rademacher multipliers generate exploratory maximum-studentized bands with 99,999 draws. A 99 percent Monte Carlo upper order statistic protects the estimated 95 percent multiplier quantile from simulation error within each family. That Monte Carlo protection does not address sampling undercoverage. I retain separate 184 Gikuriro/cash, \PairFamilyN\ all-policy, and \BoundFamilyN\ endpoint families, deduplicating affine repetitions. A zero estimated variance receives its deterministic support range rather than a structural-zero interval; \GuardedRowN\ displayed rows require this guard. Pointwise intervals use 21 block degrees of freedom. None of these choices establishes exact weak-null inference.

Synthetic tests use the actual released quota structure, 800 repeated assignments per scenario, and a 4,999-draw diagnostic multiplier calculation for fifteen mean comparisons. Table \ref{tab:stress} reports joint coverage. The equal-weight and unequal-weight failures remain material even with Monte Carlo upper quantiles. The rare-effect scenario obtains full-support intervals when its realized variance vanishes. Its full coverage cannot rescue the other scenarios. I therefore label every resulting block-region allocation and loss bound as exploratory. ANCOVA CR2 diagnostics concern a different estimator and cannot validate these ratios or missingness endpoints.

\subsection{What changes the guarantee?}

I compare the quota construction with established exponential bounds under the same weighted target, item-endpoint rules, score functions, costs and rational allocation proposals. Appendix \ref{app:methodcomparison} includes without-replacement Hoeffding, Serfling, an unrelaxed harmonic martingale bound, a weight-sensitive product bound, and an empirical-Bernstein adaptation with a fixed baseline forecast. Each alternative selects its statistical scale from baseline quantities and uses its own shared event. I separately choose support multipliers and verify the upper certificates. The inexpensive product/harmonic hybrid gives \HybridMeanCertificate\ groups and \HybridShortfallCertificate\ shortfall units, compared with \QuotaMeanLoss\ and \QuotaShortfallLoss\ for the quota procedure. Independent exact-rational adversarial distributions and a feasible policy dual now bracket the optimized loss within each implemented outer region. For quota, the mean bracket is $[\QuotaRegionalMeanLower,\QuotaRegionalMeanUpper]$ groups: the selected proposal is within \QuotaRegionalMeanGap\ groups of the regional minimax value. Thus its broad guarantee is not explained by a poor allocation search within that relaxation. This is a result about the implemented region, whose distributions need not correspond to a jointly sharp finite potential population. It proves neither a lower bound on the best possible inference procedure nor a small-loss deployment conclusion.

An ablation using the same quota moments but separate tail inequalities gives \SeparateQuotaMeanCertificate\ groups and \SeparateQuotaShortfallCertificate\ shortfall units. Thus, retaining the shared exponential constraint matters beyond improving the moment constants. The floor region is contained in the region without identified-bin floors. Combining their lower and upper brackets bounds the reduction in optimized loss from those floors by less than 0.000916 groups and 0.000446 shortfall units, with scalar consistency retained. A deterministic design catalogue compares quota and the inexpensive hybrid under common baseline searches. Its scalar-radius reductions range from 1.39 to 6.74 percent across 33 cases; the upper end occurs at a twelve-village half quota with one weight ten and eleven weights one. This limited comparison neither establishes dominance over variance-adaptive inference nor measures the value of field information.

\begin{corollary}[A fixed package share and a changing comparator]\label{cor:mandate}
For $0\leq\gamma\leq1$ and $c_g=b$, imposing $q_g\geq\gamma$ on both allocation and comparator makes the feasible set $\gamma e_g+(1-\gamma)\mathcal Q(b)$. For any fixed common mean region, the minimized own-menu regret is $(1-\gamma)$ times the original minimum.
\end{corollary}

For example, a 25 percent minimum Gikuriro share gives own-menu loss \MinimumGKOwnLoss, exactly three quarters of \TightMeanLoss. This is a geometric consequence of the comparator restriction, rather than empirical evidence favoring that mandate. Keeping the original comparator prevents this mechanical reduction from being interpreted as improved protection against foregone alternatives.

Unused expected funds require a separate choice of objective. The original criterion contains only current diet. A hypothetical value $\eta$ groups per unspent dollar changes the value to $q'\mu+\eta(b-c'q)$ and changes comparator advantages accordingly. Appendix Table \ref{tab:resourcevalues} reports several explicit values. They are not estimated welfare weights or evidence that an organization should withhold funds. Additional benefits, fiscal opportunity costs or obligations can change the allocation even if the measured diets remain fixed.

\section{Robustness and interpretation}

\subsection{Regression crosswalk and weight sensitivity}

The appendix compares the original-style ANCOVA with the current specification. Complete-case families, coarse missing-outcome calculations and influence diagnostics remain in the replication tables; they are auxiliary checks rather than additional main findings. The regression conditions on baseline transformations, a missing indicator, and assignment blocks. It imputes missing baseline controls by the weighted within-block mean and never fills a missing endline outcome for the integer analysis. Village CR1 standard errors and maximum-studentized bands are working asymptotic assessments. CR2/Satterthwaite corrections use a specified inverse-weight working covariance. These estimators have different scopes from the block ratio and finite conditional calculation.

The source-pooled crosswalk reproduces the original study's rounded Gikuriro and large-cash mean coefficients. Subsequent columns split the smaller cash arms, remove the original source-selected control list, and change the outcome and baseline measurement. No new LASSO selection is run. Equal-household and equal-village specifications also change which households or villages receive representation. Their signs provide composition sensitivity rather than independent estimates of the same weighted target. Weight concentration and village/block omission diagnostics expose influential units without making the remaining ratios selection robust.

The endline anthropometry sample combines children with different cohort histories. Source flags identify \BaselineChildCohort\ baseline-eligible children flagged for baseline anthropometry. Of these, \LinkedChildCohort\ have a linked endline row. The source endline eligibility/due flags identify \EndlineChildDue\ children, of whom \DueLinkedChildCohort\ belong to that baseline cohort and \DueOutsideChildCohort\ do not. The latter are outside the baseline-flagged cohort; the flags do not show that every one is a newborn. Table \ref{tab:childcohort} reports the partition by assignment.

Appendix \ref{app:saving} develops that possibility: a higher return to saving can raise intertemporal utility and reduce current food spending, while discrete group thresholds leave variety unchanged. Testing this explanation would require variation in saving-group access or terms separately from the nutrition, agriculture and sanitation activities. The bundled assignment supplies no such comparison, irrespective of the saving coefficient's significance.

\subsection{Deployment and omitted value}

The deployment benchmark holds eligibility, participation and package delivery at their trial values. A different mix of villages could change prices, networks or provider capacity. Those responses enter the allocation problem through outcomes and costs; estimating them would require variation in coverage beyond the original assignments. A donor's minimum-assistance rule would instead change the feasible menu directly, as in Table \ref{tab:institutional}.

For benefits outside current diet, let $H_a$ denote an additional package value in diet-equivalent units. Gikuriro exceeds lottery $q$ under that broader criterion when $H_g-\sum_aq_aH_a\geq-(\mu_g-q'\mu)$. At the fitted identified-diet means, the required differential relative to lower/large cash is \GKFittedRegret\ groups. This gives an accounting threshold for a specified extra benefit. Health outcomes, saving returns and preferences would be needed to estimate it; selection uncertainty already permits the full-baseline diet comparison to reverse sign.

\section{Discussion and conclusion}

At Gikuriro's expected provider budget, a lottery combining lower and large cash has the highest fitted mean among identified diets. It exceeds Gikuriro by \GKFittedRegret\ food groups. Assigning villages to that lottery maintains broad coverage and concentrates the larger package in \LowerLargeShare\ percent of them. The comparison uses the original participation and average-cost convention; it does not establish the dietary ranking for households whose follow-up diets remain unidentified.

The uncertainty calculation is much less decisive than the fitted ranking. Equal shares on the five individually affordable packages attain a \SupportMeanLoss-group bound using support and costs alone. Outcome consistency supplies \ConsistencyTightening\ groups of tightening; the earlier conditional finite intersection adds \FiniteIncrementOverConsistency. Using the quota moments and a shared exponential budget instead verifies a \QuotaMeanLoss-group allocation bound. This reduces the earlier \TightMeanLoss\ bound substantially with the same released data, but still allows large losses. The shortfall bound of \QuotaShortfallLoss\ is also broad relative to its unit range. These procedures establish neither a small-loss decision nor a lower limit on what every valid analysis could learn. The much smaller block approximation fails its reported coverage diagnostics.

The verified quota allocation assigns some villages to control and leaves expected resources unused. The institutional sensitivity on the earlier coherent region shows that universal assistance raises optimized regret against the original menu to \UniversalCommonLoss. A donor must specify which packages and uses of funds are admissible before applying either calculation. Changing that menu also changes the comparator; the difference between optimized bounds cannot measure the actual dietary cost of a mandate at the unknown true means.

Gikuriro's stated objective concerns maternal and child nutrition, whereas the main outcome records household food-group variety. The child follow-up regressions and saving illustration address parts of that gap but do not identify the full nutritional or intertemporal benefit of the package. Repeated dietary measures and separate variation in saving terms or program saturation could examine those responses. Original assignment documentation, provider objectives and rollout costs would also make the decision benchmark more informative. The current evidence supports conditional dietary comparisons and quantifies the remaining loss bound; it leaves the wider welfare ranking open.

\section{Decision and inference proofs}\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:vertices}.}
The distribution and objective follow from conditioning on package assignment under stable delivery and no cross-village interference. The feasible set is a compact polytope. A linear objective attains its maximum at a vertex. If the cost constraint is slack, the probability simplex permits only one positive coordinate at a vertex. If it binds, three positive coordinates allow a nonzero perturbation satisfying both the sum and cost equalities. A sufficiently small positive or negative perturbation preserves nonnegativity, contradicting extremality. Thus no vertex requires more than two packages. Equal costs or an exactly affordable pure package do not alter this upper bound. Corollary \ref{cor:coverage} follows by subtracting the control mean and using $q_L=c_g/c_L$.

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:allocation}.}
For each $\mu$, maximizing $(r-q)'\mu$ over $r$ is a linear problem whose maximum occurs at a vertex. A finite maximum commutes with the supremum over $\mathcal C$. Each resulting supremum of affine functions is convex in $q$. Compactness gives continuity and existence of a minimizer. For comparator vertex $r_j$, linear-program duality gives
\[
 \sup_{A\mu\leq d}(r_j-q)'\mu
 =\min_{\lambda_j\geq0:\ A'\lambda_j=r_j-q}d'\lambda_j.
\]
The allocation program minimizes $t$ subject to $q\in\mathcal Q(b)$ and these dual equalities with $d'\lambda_j\leq t$ for every comparator. Its constraints are linear jointly in $q,t,\lambda_j$. When the common true $\mu$ belongs to $\mathcal C$, the bound holds for every $q$ at once, including the data-selected minimizer. This coverage implication does not optimize expected regret over sampling distributions. The code independently solves the primal adversary for every comparator to check dual values and allocation feasibility.

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:projection}.}
On the protected event, each true primitive expectation lies in its stated interval. Each arm's true probability vector is nonnegative, sums to one and satisfies \eqref{eq:distributionregion}. Consistency bounds follow by bounding each unassigned household's potential transformed score by $[L_v,H_v]$ and each assigned score by its item interval. Their intersection therefore retains the true vector. Because the defined distribution polytope has no cross-arm constraints, its scalar projection is the Cartesian product of the armwise minimum/maximum intervals: compactness attains each endpoint, and convexity attains every intermediate mean. This is exact projection of the stated outer set, not a claim that each vector corresponds to a finite potential-outcome population with the given household weights. The original simultaneous event covers all transformations before projection, so no additional multiplicity adjustment is needed. Proposition \ref{prop:allocation} then bounds regret for the selected allocation on that same event.

For the potential identified-diet ratio, write $N_a$ and $D_a$ for the known weighted numerator and denominator among assigned households with identified diets, and $U_a$ for total unassigned baseline weight. Bounded unknown potential scores and observation indicators imply bounds $(N_a+L_vU_a)/(D_a+U_a)$ and $(N_a+H_vU_a)/(D_a+U_a)$ when this denominator is positive. Both endpoints are attained in the unrestricted scalar relaxation by observing all unassigned households at a support endpoint. Assigned unidentified diets remain excluded even when a particular transform is known. These scalar inequalities are additional valid outer restrictions, not joint-sharpness or common-population selection assumptions.

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:quota}.}
For fixed $W_j$, $G_b(y,u)$ is affine jointly in $y$ and $u$. Its average exponential over quota subsets is convex on $[0,1]^{n_b+1}$. Successively maximizing each coordinate on its interval shows that a maximum occurs at a binary vertex. Equation \eqref{eq:quotamoment} therefore bounds the positive moment of every centered block residual. Replacing $(y,u)$ by $(1-y,1-u)$ reverses $G_b$, so it also bounds the negative moment. Independent blocks multiply their moment bounds. The population mean identity cancels the across-block centering, leaving the arm's realized Horvitz--Thompson residual $S_{az}$. Consequently $\E\exp\{\pm\lambda_a S_{az}-B_a\}\leq1$.

For every assignment, monotone item intervals imply
\[
 N_{az}^--\widehat D_a u_{az}\leq S_{az}\leq N_{az}^+-\widehat D_a u_{az},\qquad u_{az}=z'p_a^{\rm true}.
\]
Thus both computable terms in \eqref{eq:quotatests} are no greater than their corresponding true-residual terms. Each has expectation at most one without an assumption that missing diets are ignorable. Dependence among arms, transformations or their missingness patterns does not affect the expectation of their sum. Markov's inequality gives probability at most $276/5520=0.05$ that it exceeds 5520. The true score distributions also satisfy unit mass, nonnegativity, consistency and a bin floor equal to the fixed-weight share actually assigned to that arm with an identified score. Intersecting these deterministic restrictions preserves coverage. On that one event, the common true distributions bound regret for every feasible $q$ and every specified linear score objective. Data-dependent selection of shares or support dual variables incurs no further test selection.

\paragraph{Outward constants and support verification.}
For numerical evaluation of \eqref{eq:quotamoment}, the sorted-threshold recurrence uses the actual stored weights. Directed Decimal arithmetic encloses the exponent arguments, correctly rounded elementary functions are enlarged by adjacent Decimal values, and positive recurrence coefficients propagate the upper bounds. Appendix \ref{app:quotacomputation} gives the construction. The earlier exhaustive integer-proxy implementation remains available as a separate reference, including its explicit weight-drift allowance; the current calculation needs no proxy rounding. Directed assignment-weight arithmetic supplies a lower endpoint numerator, an upper endpoint numerator and denominator enclosures. The stored affine log tests are no greater than the conceptual tests for any nonnegative probability vector. The normalized score entries themselves are fixed bounded functions; no exact floating-point identity with the raw objective is presumed.

Write $F_a(p_a)$ for the stored arm sum and $C=5520$. For any $\tau\geq0$ and direction $d$,
\[
 \sup_{\sum_aF_a\leq C,\ p_a\in\mathcal P_a^{\rm obs}}d'p
 \leq\tau+\sum_a\sup_{p_a\in\mathcal P_a^{\rm obs}}\{d_a'p_a-\tau F_a(p_a)/C\}.
\]
The negative arm objective is convex. At any candidate, interval-enclosed values and gradients produce an affine tangent lower bound, with its gradient enclosure error bounded over $0\leq p_{ad}\leq1$. The tangent's minimum is bounded below using a feasible LP dual, rather than a numerical primal objective minus an arbitrary cushion. If the logical polytope is $p\geq f$, $Ap\leq b$, $\boldsymbol1'p=1$, any $\ell\leq0$ and $\zeta$ satisfying $A'\ell+\zeta\boldsymbol1\leq g$ give
\[
 \min g'p\geq g'f+\ell'(b-Af)+\zeta(1-\boldsymbol1'f).
\]
The code clamps suggested inequality multipliers to nonpositive values, constructs an outward feasible equality multiplier and evaluates this expression downward. It encloses score-direction rounding and uses exact rational allocation shares and ideal cost vertices. Taking the largest of the nine resulting support upper bounds verifies the selected allocation. Upward decimal rounding gives the displayed values. None of these steps relies on global success of the numerical minimizer or proves that the selected allocation is the exact minimax solution. The audit replays all tangents and checks dual feasibility independently with exact fractions.

\paragraph{Proof of Corollary \ref{cor:mandate}.}
For $\gamma<1$, every $q$ with $q_g\geq\gamma$ can be uniquely written as $\gamma e_g+(1-\gamma)s$. Nonnegativity and the sum restriction hold exactly when $s$ is a simplex vector. Since $c_g=b$, its cost inequality is equivalent to $c's\leq b$. The same transformation applies to each comparator, so their difference is $(1-\gamma)(r-s)$. Taking the supremum and minimum scales the regret by $1-\gamma$. For $\gamma=1$, the class contains only Gikuriro and own-menu regret is zero. Nothing in this identity ranks restricted policies against excluded alternatives.

\paragraph{Finite conditional-assignment interval.}
Fix an arm, a primitive outcome supported on $[L,H]$, and its target
$\mu=\sum_iw_iR_i(a)Y_i(a)/\sum_iw_iR_i(a)$, assuming a positive potential denominator. For complete baseline endpoints $R_i(a)=1$. Define village residuals
\[
 X_j(\mu)=\sum_{i\in j}w_iR_i(a)\{Y_i(a)-\mu\}.
\]
Because $\mu\in[L,H]$, every residual in block $b$ lies in $[(L-\mu)W_b^{\max},(H-\mu)W_b^{\max}]$, including villages with unobserved outcomes or a smaller total weight. This interval has width $(H-L)W_b^{\max}$. Its inverse-probability-scaled width is $(H-L)W_b^{\max}/p_{ba}$.

Under exchangeable quota assignment, the arm's residual sum in a block is a simple random sample of size $k_{ba}$ from the fixed village residuals divided by $p_{ba}$. Theorem 4 of \citet{hoeffding1963} bounds the convex exponential moment of this without-replacement sum by the with-replacement moment. Applying the bounded-variable exponential inequality to those $k_{ba}$ independent draws, and multiplying across independent blocks, gives
\[
 \Pr\!\left(\left|\sum_j\frac{T_{ja}X_j(\mu)}{p_{b(j),a}}\right|>t\right)
 \leq 2\exp\left\{-\frac{2t^2}{(H-L)^2V_a}\right\}.
\]
The total residual expectation is zero since $\sum_jX_j(\mu)=0$. Substituting \eqref{eq:finite} makes this failure probability at most $\alpha/M$. The realized residual is $\widehat D_a(\widehat\mu_a-\mu)$. Dividing by its positive realized denominator therefore yields the reported ratio interval. This inversion tests the fixed true $\mu$; it needs no union over a grid of candidate means. A zero realized denominator receives full support. A union bound protects the $M$ fixed primitive arm/transformation/endpoints. Applying deterministic endpoint ordering then covers true missing-diet means and every policy computed from the same primitive region. Dependence across arms is allowed in the union bound; independent blocks and conditional exchangeability remain essential. The bound is classical and conservative, not an optimal confidence procedure.

\paragraph{Block covariance and the referee counterexample.}
For independent population linearization scores $\psi_b$ with means $m_b$ satisfying $\sum_bm_b=0$, the expectation of their centered block covariance is
\[
 \E\left[\frac{B}{B-1}\sum_b(\psi_b-\bar\psi)(\psi_b-\bar\psi)'\right]
 =\sum_b\operatorname{Var}(\psi_b)+\frac{B}{B-1}\sum_bm_bm_b'.
\]
The excess is positive semidefinite. Estimated ratio denominators and centering require an asymptotic linearization, so this identity alone proves no finite-sample coverage. In a ten-village block with five fixed scores 4 and five 6 for every arm, assign one village each to lower and upper cash without replacement. Exhausting all 90 ordered pairs gives covariance $-1/(9B)$ between the two arm means across $B$ independent blocks. The true difference variance is $20/(9B)$; discarding cross-arm dependence instead gives $2/B$, only 90 percent of that variance. The common block vector retains the missing covariance. The synthetic coverage failures in Table \ref{tab:stress} show why repairing this algebra is insufficient to validate the subsequent multiplier approximation with 22 blocks.

\paragraph{Distributional priorities.}
On the finite integer support, any increasing function is its value at zero plus a nonnegative combination of survival indicators. Increasing-concave functions equivalently have nonnegative weights on truncated linear gains. Thus simultaneous distributional ordering would imply the corresponding class of preferences over this proxy. The empirical regions do not establish such an ordering. Preference over HDDS is distinct from preference over nutrients or full intertemporal resources.

\section{An illustrative saving mechanism}\label{app:saving}
\subsection{Household allocation}

A household has current resources $w+t$, where $w$ is its initial budget and $t$ is a transfer. It allocates resources to current food expenditure $f$ and saving $s$. Food is measured in units with price normalized to one. Let $\underline f$ denote subsistence expenditure, $R$ the positive gross return on saving, $y>0$ background future income, and $\beta>0$ the weight on future consumption. For an interior household with $w+t>\underline f$, preferences and the budget constraint are
\begin{equation}
 U(f,s)=\log(f-\underline f)+\beta\log(Rs+y),\qquad
 f+s=w+t,\quad s\geq0. \label{eq:household}
\end{equation}
The future resource $y$ is important: it allows a change in the saving return to affect the allocation between dates. The model abstracts from risk, credit, household bargaining, and the multiple activities provided by Gikuriro. Its comparative statics describe a possible mechanism within that bundle.

Food diversity is an increasing step function of spending,
\begin{equation}
 D(f)=\sum_{j=1}^{12}\ind\{f\geq a_j\}, \qquad
 \underline f\leq a_1\leq\cdots\leq a_{12}, \label{eq:diet}
\end{equation}
where $a_j$ are household-specific thresholds for including additional food groups. This reduced form allows a household to maintain a staple diet while deferring more expensive sources of variety. It does not imply that each food group has the same nutrient content or that variety depends only on total expenditure.

\begin{proposition}[Current food and saving]\label{prop:saving}
If the optimum is interior, then
\[
 f^*=\frac{w+t+y/R+\beta\underline f}{1+\beta},\qquad
 s^*=\frac{\beta(w+t-\underline f)-y/R}{1+\beta}.
\]
Cash increases both current food expenditure and saving. A rise in $R$ decreases current food expenditure, increases saving, and increases maximized household utility. Dietary diversity weakly increases with cash, but can remain unchanged over an interval of transfer values.
\end{proposition}

If saving is constrained at zero, a small change in its return has no effect on allocation; cash initially finances current spending. The saving-return prediction holds $y$ fixed. I do not estimate $R$, $y$, or $\beta$: saving groups may change fees, credit, risk or commitments instead. The model illustrates how current food access and intertemporal utility can rank an intervention differently.

\begin{corollary}[A short observation window]
A saving intervention can raise household utility while leaving the observed diversity score unchanged, or reducing it. A small cash transfer can raise food spending without increasing the diversity score. A larger transfer crosses a threshold only if an uncrossed threshold is reachable; a household already consuming all twelve groups cannot increase this score.
\end{corollary}

The bundled assignment cannot distinguish this channel from delivery timing or other program components. Repeated measurements or randomized mechanism variation would be needed.


\paragraph{Proof of Proposition \ref{prop:saving}.}
Substitution of $s=w+t-f$ gives the first-order condition
$1/(f-\underline f)=\beta R/(Rs+y)$. Strict concavity gives the unique interior optimum stated in the proposition. Its derivatives are $\partial f^*/\partial t=1/(1+\beta)$, $\partial s^*/\partial t=\beta/(1+\beta)$, and $\partial f^*/\partial R=-y/\{(1+\beta)R^2\}<0$. The saving derivative has the opposite sign. The envelope derivative of maximized utility is $\beta s^*/(Rs^*+y)>0$ at an interior positive-saving optimum. At the zero-saving boundary, the allocation response to a small return change is zero. Dietary diversity is a step function; transfer increases weakly raise it and may leave it unchanged. Once all twelve thresholds are crossed, the ceiling prevents a further score gain. No $R$, $y$, or $\beta$ is calibrated from the bundled reduced form.

\section{Established-method comparisons and component ablations}\label{app:methodcomparison}

This appendix assesses which implementation choices change the empirical guarantee while preserving the same weighted released-baseline target. Averaging expectation-bounded nonnegative tests is established e-value theory \citep{vovk2021}; the shared aggregation is not a new theorem. Range comparisons use \citet{bardenet2015}, including the harmonic sum in the proof of Proposition 2.2. I also implement a weight-sensitive product bound and a restricted empirical-Bernstein adaptation of \citet{waudby2020}. The latter uses fixed forecasts and baseline-selected scales. It is not their complete adaptive-scale procedure, and the comparisons do not establish superiority over modern betting methods.

For one block write $t_j=W_j(y_j-u)$, $W_{\max}=\max_jW_j$, and $G=(n/k)\sum_{j\in A}t_j-\sum_jt_j$. Since $0\leq y_j,u\leq1$, the $t_j$ have range at most $W_{\max}$. Under uniform quota sampling, the standard exponential inequalities imply $\log\E e^{\lambda G}\leq\lambda^2 V/8$, with forward coefficients
\[
\frac{V_H}{W_{\max}^2}=\frac{n^2}{k},\qquad
\frac{V_S}{W_{\max}^2}=\frac{n^2}{k}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{n}\right),\qquad
\frac{V_M}{W_{\max}^2}=\frac{n^2(n-k)^2}{k^2}\sum_{t=1}^k\frac{1}{(n-t)^2}.
\]
The harmonic expression applies for $k<n$; at $k=n$ the residual is zero. Complementing the sampled subset gives $G_k=-(n-k)G_{n-k}/k$, so I use the smaller forward or rescaled complementary coefficient. Selection uses baseline design quantities alone. Independent blocks add their coefficients.

For completeness, choosing predictable scales $\lambda_t=\Lambda(n-k)/(n-t)$ in the without-replacement Hoeffding process, with $\Lambda=\lambda n/k$, makes the coefficient on sample position $j$ equal to
\[
\lambda_j+\sum_{t=j+1}^k\frac{\lambda_t}{n-t+1}=\Lambda.
\]
Its centered outcome sum is therefore $\lambda G$; its quadratic compensation is the displayed harmonic coefficient. This retains an established martingale calculation before the integral relaxation leading to Serfling's simpler bound. It is not a new concentration inequality.

Every field alternative uses the same item intervals, 23 normalized score functions, deterministic restrictions, costs and rational proposals. Its 276 expectation-bounded terms yield a standalone conditional 95 percent shared event. Classical scales minimize their quadratic radius analytically; product and hybrid scales use a baseline-only scalar search; the quota procedure retains its four-point baseline grid. The empirical-Bernstein procedure instead compensates the realized village scores and uses the fixed population denominator. Support scales may depend on outcomes because they certify deterministic bounds within an event. No observed minimum across separately valid events is asserted as a jointly valid 95 percent guarantee.

\subsection{Certified regional optimization brackets}
The displayed lower bounds concern the implemented outer sets defined by the stored outward affine tests, logical rows and bin floors. For each method I construct 19 exact-rational probability populations, including an interior seed and candidates informed by the existing support searches. Unit mass, nonnegativity, floors and logical inequalities are checked with exact fractions. A directed exponential sum no greater than 5520 certifies membership in the implemented event. Floating searches choose displacements toward the seed; only the final directed membership check admits a population. No numerical feasibility tolerance admits one.

Let $\mu^m$ be its exact arm-mean vector and let each index $\ell$ pair a feasible population with a budget-feasible comparator vertex $r_\ell$. Put $a_\ell=r_\ell'\mu^m$. Any nonnegative probabilities $w_\ell$ summing to one and any $\eta\geq0$ give, for every $q\in\mathcal Q(b)$,
\[
 \max_\ell\{a_\ell-(\mu^m)'q\}
 \geq\sum_\ell w_\ell a_\ell-\eta b
 -\max_j\left\{\sum_\ell w_\ell\mu^m_j-\eta c_j\right\}.
\]
The first inequality averages the finite adversary's losses; the second uses $c'q\leq b$, nonnegative shares and unit mass. I extract candidate probabilities and a cost multiplier from a finite policy LP, normalize and clamp them to be feasible, and evaluate this expression exactly. Its nonnegative part is a regional minimax lower bound. A separately verified feasible proposal supplies the upper bound. Neither a floating primal value nor a purported optimizer gap supplies either certificate.

\subsection{Computing the relaxed quota moment}\label{app:quotacomputation}
For $u=0$, write $x_j=W_jy_j\in[0,W_j]$ and
\[
 F(x)=\binom nk^{-1}\sum_{|A|=k}\exp\left\{\lambda\left(\frac nk\sum_{j\in A}x_j-\sum_jx_j\right)\right\}.
\]
This is symmetric and convex. Fix a maximizing binary vertex with $m$ active coordinates. If an active weight $W_i$ is below an inactive weight $W_j$, remove the active coordinate and regard its value as a scalar argument $s$ of the resulting symmetric convex function $g(s)$. Global maximality gives $g(W_i)\geq g(0)$. Convex secant slopes imply $g(W_j)\geq g(W_i)$, so exchanging the active indices preserves a maximum. Repeating exchanges leaves the $m$ largest weights active. The same argument holds for the negative sign; $u=1$ reduces to that sign by complementation. Thus $2(n+1)$ sorted populations suffice. General symmetric-convex range computation under interval uncertainty predates this application \citep{xiang2006}. The argument here supplies a self-contained specialization, not a claim to a new general algorithm.

For a sorted population with total $S$, put $a_j=\exp\{s\lambda(nx_j-S)/k\}$, where $s\in\{-1,1\}$. Its quota average is the elementary symmetric mean, computed by
\[
 D_{t,r}=\frac{t-r}{t}D_{t-1,r}+\frac rt a_tD_{t-1,r-1},\qquad D_{t,0}=1.
\]
The terminal value $D_{n,k}$ is the average of the products over all $k$-subsets. Complementation permits evaluation at $q=\min(k,n-k)$; the exponent denominator remains the original $k$. Directed argument, exponential, positive-coefficient recurrence and logarithm evaluations enclose the maximum. The arithmetic cost is $O(n^2q)$ with $O(n+q)$ working storage. An independent 160-digit full population/subset calculation checks the implementation on small fixtures; the exhaustive proxy program remains available.

The declared computation catalogue contains 16 designs, with block sizes 12, 24, 48 and 96, quarter/half quotas, and equal/concentrated weights. It times three actual serial calls per method at a fixed scale. Exhaustive computation is skipped when $2^n\binom nk$ exceeds four million, with no extrapolated timing claim. The 96-village half-quota calculations take roughly two seconds on the recorded Windows interpreter; the inexpensive hybrid remains substantially faster. Timings in the machine-readable receipt concern moments, not end-to-end allocation optimization.

\subsection{Weight-sensitive product and empirical compensation}
Let $p=k/n$ and $I_j=\ind\{j\in A\}$. For nonnegative $a_j$, expanding the product and using $\Pr(S\subset A)=(k)_{|S|}/(n)_{|S|}\leq p^{|S|}$ gives $\E\prod_j(1+a_jI_j)\leq\prod_j(1+pa_j)$. Complementary inclusion indicators give the negative-sign analogue. This is the familiar negative-association comparison \citep{joagdev1983}. Convex endpoint reduction and monotonicity in nonnegative amplitudes yield
\[
 B_P=\max_{s\in\{-1,1\}}\sum_j\log\left\{(1-p)e^{-s\lambda W_j}+pe^{s\lambda(n-k)W_j/k}\right\}.
\]
The minimum of $B_P$ and the harmonic quadratic bound still bounds the same block moment. Because it is fixed from baseline design quantities, using that minimum does not select or intersect observed confidence events. At a half quota the product simplifies to $\sum_j\log\cosh(\lambda W_j)$.

For the empirical-Bernstein comparison, normalize $X_j=W_jy_j/W_{\max}\in[0,1]$ and fix a forecast $\gamma=\sum_jW_j/(2nW_{\max})$. Use predictable scales $\theta_t=\lambda(n/k)W_{\max}(n-k)/(n-t)<1$ and compensation $\rho(\theta)=-\log(1-\theta)-\theta$. The published supermartingale argument \citep{waudby2020} permits a predictable bounded forecast. The telescoping coefficients give
\[
 M_s=\exp\left\{s\lambda G(y,0)-\sum_{t=1}^k\rho(\theta_t)(X_{A_t}-\gamma)^2\right\},\qquad \E M_s\leq1.
\]
For either sign, each observed contribution is concave in $X_{A_t}$, so its minimum over the item interval occurs at an endpoint. Directed endpoint minima give a pathwise lower test without an ignorability condition. The field implementation averages the positive tests over all $k!$ orders of the observed villages; it does not presume that village identifiers were randomly ordered. Independent blocks multiply these averages. Their population factors cancel at the full weighted mean, leaving affine observable log tests with coefficient $\pm\lambda N$, where $N$ is fixed total weight. The 276 tests therefore have the same Markov failure bound of 0.05. The baseline scale heuristic assumes a uniform unit score only to choose an admissible scale; actual compensation uses the observed endpoints. Fixed forecasts, the common scale, order averaging and conservative endpoint minimization limit this adaptation. Its field bounds, \EmpBernMeanCertificate\ and \EmpBernShortfallCertificate, cannot be taken as evidence against the full variance-adaptive or betting family.
